    Roadmap

    Updated 8 November 2026, after the Vesta landing.

    KOSMOS: the Kerbal Organization for Socialist Missions, Orbits and Science. Formerly the KCSP.

    KOSMOS has landed on another planet. Tianwen-1 carried Pechka 1 to Vesta, where it touched down in the Abyss region, returned 193 science units and a surface photograph, and kept a radio link home for about three hours before the planet hid its relay. The program also leads on reuse (B1002 retired after 10 flights) and holds Kir station and the Iota base. The next race is in orbit: the Americans are close to their first docking, so Kir's refuelling depot is the priority.

    Intelligence on the Americans

    The Americans are a real rival, not a dormant one. Photographs of their VAB show about 1.92 million funds (as much as ours), two working launcher families (Harrier, a copy of the Atlas V 421, and Kestrel), and a modular uncrewed station series called Horizon (Junction and Lantern modules). They are now close to their first docking with advanced docking ports, and run a more developed mission control panel than ours. Nothing in the photographs leaves their home planet's orbit.

    Before every launch

    • Open Mission Control. Accept every contract this flight can finish on the way.
    • Pair cheap satellite contracts with a bigger launch: the Gael polar pair, and the Iota pair.
    • Look for contracts close to expiring that need no new craft (First Flight, the pressure survey).
    • Before a big Party mission with no customer, take one paying job first so the program's funds keep growing.

    Next up: refuel at Kir before the Americans dock

    The Americans are close to their first docking, with advanced docking ports and a fuel-depot design (Reservoir) on paper. KOSMOS already docks at Kir, so the feat still open to both sides is the first orbital refuelling.

    1. Dock Kir Fuel Module 1 to Kir.
    2. Fly a Phoenix 10 tanker and transfer fuel into the module.
    3. Rotate Kir's crew on the Kir Station Return Pod so the station stays continuously crewed.

    Niven stays the next planetary target: the same second stage with a light Kerblink-bus orbiter makes the 1,010 m/s Niven departure.

    Kir station: growth plan

    Kir orbits at 115-126 km with an 8.7 degree inclination, so every Kerblink shell stays below 110 km (groups 1-7 at 85-109 km) and crossing traffic stays clear.

    • Turn Kir into the program's fuel depot: dock Kir Fuel Module 1, then refill it with Phoenix 10 tanker flights. A full depot makes the crewed Niven trip possible without a bigger rocket.
    • Rotate crews with the Kir Station Return Pod and keep at least one Kerbal aboard; a continuous presence is a feat the Americans cannot match while their program is dormant.
    • Add a science lab module so Iota and Ceti samples are processed in orbit.
    • At 374 parts Kir costs frame rate whenever a craft is near it. Future modules should be few large parts rather than many small ones.

    The Ciro system as our astronomers know it

    A briefing written as the program's observatories would give it today, with no probe yet beyond Gael's moons: the near planets are well measured by radar and telescope, but past Otho the figures are estimates, and some are probably wrong. Distances are in Gael units (GU), Gael's distance from Ciro (14.0 Gm).

    BodyDistance (GU)What we knowConfidence
    Grannus (second star)0.09A red companion star of about half Ciro's mass, circling Ciro every few days; three planets of its own: Taranis, Toutatis (atmosphere seen in spectra) and CernunnosGood
    Icarus0.25Small (320 km), airless, scorched; turns once every 213 hours, so its day lasts weeksGood: radar-mapped
    Vesta0.50The "hearth star": a dazzling white point in the evening sky, wrapped in cloud no telescope has ever seen through. Old almanacs still list it as Thalia, an airless rock about 540 km across; newer radar puts it at about 1,140 km across, nearly Gael's size, with nine-tenths of Gael's gravity, and the clouds are real. Pulp serials put steaming swamps and fern forests under that veil, but radio astronomers insist the ground is hotter than a kitchen oven, so it was renamed Vesta, for the goddess whose sacred fire never goes out. Sailors call it unlucky. One small icy moon, Eta.Poor (clouds hide the surface)
